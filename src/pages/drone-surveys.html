What you get from a drone survey

  • A scaled orthophoto: one seamless, distortion-free aerial image of the whole site, at around 1–2 cm per pixel, that you can measure from like a plan.
  • A surface model with levels: every part of the site has a level, not just the points someone chose to pick up.
  • Contours and spot levels in DWG at the interval your designer or engineer needs.
  • A point cloud and 3D model for volumes, sections and anything nobody thought to ask for on the day.
  • A plain accuracy statement: how many control and check points were used, and how far the model was from the check points.

Why ground control matters

A drone's own GPS is good enough to fly the grid, not to survey with. Left uncorrected, an aerial model can be scaled slightly wrong, tilted, or a metre or more out in position and level, and it will still look perfect on screen. That's the difference between a nice aerial picture and a survey.

Before we fly, we lay out ground control targets around the site and measure each one with an RTK GNSS rover. The model is fixed to those points. We also measure extra check points that are kept out of the processing, then compare the finished model with them. If it doesn't meet the accuracy the job needs, we don't issue it.

Where a drone survey is the right tool

Open land and sites over about a quarter of an acre, earthworks and stockpiles, progress records, roofs and anything that would take days to walk with a rover. For the millimetre checks on sub-base, tarmac, slabs and brickwork we still use the laser scanner, and on a lot of jobs the two work together.

Who books drone surveys

  • Groundworks and civils contractors pricing earthworks or recording what was there before they started.
  • Housebuilders and developers appraising land and planning layouts.
  • Architects and engineers who need a site plan and levels over a large area.
  • Landowners, farms and equestrian yards mapping land, tracks, drainage and areas.

How a drone survey works

  1. Site and purpose

    A location plan or red line, what the survey is for, and the grid and datum you need.

  2. Airspace and price

    We check airspace and any restrictions, plan the flight and confirm a fixed price.

  3. Control and flight

    Ground control and check points measured with RTK GNSS, then the grid flown in one visit.

  4. Process and check

    Model built, checked against the check points, and drawn up into the deliverables you asked for.

Limits worth knowing

Photogrammetry measures what the camera sees. Ground under dense trees, standing crops or water isn't captured; we fill those areas with GNSS levels or a scan and mark them on the plan. Heavy rain, strong wind and low cloud stop flying, and we rebook rather than fly a survey that won't meet the accuracy. Flights follow the Civil Aviation Authority's rules, so a few sites close to airports need permission before we can fly.
