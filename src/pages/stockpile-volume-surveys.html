What the stockpile report shows

  • Each pile named and outlined on the aerial image, so there's no argument about which heap was which.
  • Volume in cubic metres for every pile, and a site total by material.
  • Tonnage using your supplier's or weighbridge density, or a stated typical figure.
  • How the base was set for each pile, which is where most volume disagreements start.
  • Change since last time when piles are surveyed on a regular programme.

Why measure stockpiles properly

On a groundworks job, the stone, topsoil and muck sitting on site is money. Over-order Type 1 and it's wasted; under-order and a gang stands waiting. Muck-away loads get disputed. Topsoil gets stripped, stored and respread, and nobody's quite sure whether there's enough to go back on the gardens. Estimating piles by eye or by counting wagon loads is where those numbers drift.

A controlled drone survey measures every pile in the same way, every time, without anyone climbing on them. It's quick enough to do monthly, so the numbers can sit alongside valuations, material reconciliations and cost reports.

The base is the hard part

Measuring the top of a pile is easy. Knowing what's under it is not. Where piles sit on a known, flat surface we use that. Where they were tipped onto rough ground, we build the base from the levels around the toe or, best of all, from a survey of the ground before tipping started. We tell you which method was used for each pile.

Typical stockpiles we measure

How a stockpile survey works

  1. Tell us the site

    Where the piles are, roughly how many, which materials and any densities you already have.

  2. Control

    Ground control targets placed on stable ground around the piles and measured with RTK GNSS.

  3. Fly

    The piles are flown in a grid and an orbit to capture the sides properly. No one goes on them.

  4. Report

    Volumes and tonnages per pile on a marked-up aerial image, usually the next working day.

When a drone isn't the right tool

Piles inside buildings, under roofs or fully sheeted need a laser scan from the ground, which we can also do. Piles that are being worked while we fly change shape mid-survey, so we agree a short window with the site. Very small heaps of a few cubic metres are quicker to measure with the rover. If tonnage matters commercially, a measured bulk density for your material will always beat a typical figure.
