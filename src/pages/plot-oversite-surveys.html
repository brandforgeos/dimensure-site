The three checks on every plot

1. Slab flatness and level consistency

The whole slab or floor surface is compared with a best-fit plane. The heat-map shows high and low areas, their size and depth, so you can see whether the floor screed or finish will take it up or whether it needs grinding or levelling. This checks the slab against itself; level to site datum is your engineer's.

2. Brickwork and blockwork to the house-type drawing

External and internal wall lengths, widths and diagonals at DPC, compared with the drawing. Out-of-square plots, short walls and openings in the wrong place show up as numbers, not opinions. These are the dimensions the kit, frame or joist supplier is relying on.

3. Drain collar positions

Every soil, waste and rainwater collar coming through the slab is picked out of the scan and its centre compared with the drainage layout. A collar in the wrong place is a cheap fix at oversite and a very expensive one once the kitchen or bathroom is fitted.

What we don't check

  • Plot position on the site grid or relative to neighbouring plots.
  • Finished floor level against site datum.
  • Below-ground drainage runs and falls (we see the collars, not the pipes).

Being clear about this matters. A report is only useful if everyone knows what it does and doesn't prove.

Why scan oversites at all?

Oversite is the last point where the groundworker's work is fully visible. After it the frame or the first lift goes on, floors are covered, and the plot belongs to someone else's programme. Any defect found later becomes an argument about who caused it and who pays.

A dated scan settles that argument. It shows the plot exactly as it was handed over, measured to the millimetre. For groundworkers it is evidence the oversite was right. For housebuilders it is a consistent check across every plot, done the same way by the same method, instead of depending on who was on site with a tape that day.

It also fits around the job. We scan a run of plots in one visit, set up clear of the gang and the plant, and are gone before the next trade needs the plot.

What the plot report looks like

One page per plot, written for the person who has to act on it.

  1. Summary

    Plot number, house type, scan date and an overall pass or fail.

  2. Slab

    Flatness heat-map, worst high and low, and the area outside tolerance.

  3. Brickwork

    Each measured dimension beside the drawing figure and the difference.

  4. Collars

    Each collar's offset from the drawing, flagged if it is outside your tolerance.
