What the survey shows

  • A deviation map of the whole wall face, coloured by how far each part is out of plumb.
  • Overall lean: how far the top is from the bottom, at regular points along the wall.
  • Bulges: where the wall bellies out, how big the bulge is and its exact position.
  • Sections through the worst areas, so the profile of the wall is clear on paper.
  • A baseline for monitoring, with reference points so later surveys line up exactly.

Why scan instead of a plumb bob

Plumb and tape readings are taken at a few places someone picked. A bulge between them goes unnoticed, and readings taken months apart by different people rarely line up. A scan measures the entire face, and a repeat scan from the same reference points can be compared directly with the first. That's what makes it useful for deciding whether a wall is stable or still on the move.

Who books wall surveys

Structural engineers and building surveyors

Measured evidence of the lean and bulge to base an assessment on, without spending your site visit with a plumb line.

Homeowners

When a garden wall or the side of the house doesn't look right, measuring it is the first step. You'll get clear drawings to take to an engineer or your insurer.

Contractors working next to a neighbour

A baseline before you dig or pile beside an existing wall, and repeat scans during the work, protect you if movement is claimed later.

Landlords and managing agents

A dated record of boundary and retaining walls across a property, repeated periodically to keep an eye on the ones that matter.

What it doesn't cover

We measure the wall. We don't diagnose why it's moving, design repairs or say whether it's safe; that's for a structural engineer. For the floors inside a building that's moving, see floor level surveys.
