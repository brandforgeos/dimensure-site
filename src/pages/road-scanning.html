Why scan a road layer instead of dipping it?

A dip check with a level and staff, or a GPS rover, measures a point every few metres along the centreline and channels. That is a sample. If the high spot sits between two readings, nobody finds it until the binder is down and the finished level is wrong, or until water sits in the channel after adoption.

A laser scan measures the whole layer. Hundreds of thousands of points a second, kerb face to kerb face, including the bits nobody dips: around ironwork, at tie-ins, across turning heads and at the back of footpaths. The comparison with design then runs across all of it.

What we check on a road scan

  • Sub-base or formation surface level against the design surface, with the deviation at every point.
  • Crossfall and camber across the carriageway, and channel falls towards gullies.
  • Footpath and verge levels against the back of kerb.
  • Ironwork (manhole covers, gully gratings) relative to the layer around it.
  • Kerb line and kerb upstand where it is on the drawing.

What you get back

  • A PDF report with pass/fail per section, the maximum high and low, and the percentage of the surface within tolerance.
  • A colour heat-map of the layer over the layout drawing, so the gang can go straight to the area that needs trimming or topping up.
  • Cross sections at your chainages, or every 10 m if you don't specify.
  • The registered point cloud (E57 or LAS) for your engineer, and the scan registration error so you know how tight the data is.

Tolerances: what counts as a pass

We check against the tolerance in your specification or your adopting authority's estate road guide. They differ between councils, so we always ask. As one published example, Lincolnshire County Council's estate road build tolerances are:

LayerSurface level toleranceLayer thickness tolerance
Sub-base+10 / −30 mm+40 / −0 mm
Binder course±6 mm±6 mm
Surface course±6 mm±6 mm

Example only. Your section 38 agreement or specification sets the tolerance we report against.

The tight figures are on the bituminous layers, which is exactly why the sub-base matters. Every millimetre the sub-base is high comes straight off the binder, and an under-thickness binder is a defect the adoption engineer can ask you to take up. Checking the sub-base surface is the cheapest point in the job to put it right.

How a road scan visit runs

  1. Before the visit we take your design and control, and build the design surface.
  2. On site we set up on your control or put down our own targets tied to it, then leapfrog the scanner along the road, usually every 15 to 25 metres.
  3. Same evening the scans are registered, checked and compared with design.
  4. Next working day you get the report and heat-map, ready for your engineer or the gang.

We work under your site induction and RAMS, wear the right PPE, and keep the scanner and tripod out of plant routes. Scanning is safe around people: the X9 uses an eye-safe Class 1 laser.

Where road scanning fits in

Most contractors use a road scan at one or more of these hold points:

  1. Formation

    Confirm dig depth before the membrane and stone go in, and record the volume taken out.

  2. Sub-base

    The main check. Trim or top up before the binder crew arrives.

  3. Binder

    Record the binder before it's covered for years, and capture its thickness if we scanned the sub-base.

  4. Surface course

    Final levels and thickness for adoption, and a record if there's ever a dispute.
